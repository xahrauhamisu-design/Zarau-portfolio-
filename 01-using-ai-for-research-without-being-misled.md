# How I Use AI for Research Without Getting Misled

AI tools can save hours of research time. They can also hand you a confident answer that is wrong.
I use ChatGPT and Claude in my writing work, and over time I have settled on a short routine that
keeps me honest.

## 1. Tell the tool who the answer is for

Early on, I asked a chatbot to help with a research summary and got back a paragraph so dense that
I could not follow it myself. It was not wrong. It was written for the wrong reader.

When I rewrote the prompt, I added three things: who would read the summary, the setting of the
research (Nigeria), and the level of language I wanted. The next answer was clear enough to use.

## 2. Tell it what to leave out

AI tools like to fill gaps. I add one line to most research prompts:

> If you cannot support a claim with a reliable source, say so. Do not guess.

This does not fix everything, but it cuts down on invented details.

## 3. Ask for sources, then open them

A source named by an AI can be wrong, or it may not exist. I open every link or paper it gives me
and check two things: that it is real, and that it says what the AI claimed.

## 4. Check numbers, names and dates against the original

These are the details most likely to be wrong, and the ones readers remember. I find the original
document and compare.

## 5. Keep one human pass at the end

Before I publish anything, I read it once only for facts and ask: *can I stand behind every claim?*
If the answer is no, the claim goes out or gets fixed.

## A prompt I reuse

```text
Explain [topic] for [audience] in [country or setting].
Use plain language at a [level] reading level.
Give me the main points first, then the details.
List the sources you used. If you cannot support a claim with a reliable source,
say so instead of guessing.
```

## Why this matters for technical writing

Readers of technical articles trust the writer to have checked. One wrong claim can make them doubt
the rest. AI makes writing faster, but the checking is still my job.
